Abstract Currently sparse signal reconstruction gains considerable interest and is applied in many ﬁelds. In
this paper, a similarity induced by joint sparse representation is designed to construct the likelihood function
of particle ﬁlter tracker so that the color visual spectrum and thermal spectrum images can be fused for object
tracking. The proposed fusion scheme performs joint sparse representation calculation on both modalities and
the resultant tracking results are fused using min operation on the sparse representation coeﬃcients. In addition,
a co-learning approach is proposed to update the reference templates of both modality and enhance the tracking
robustness. The proposed fusion scheme outperforms state-of-the-art approaches, and its eﬀectiveness is veriﬁed
using OTCBVS database.
Keywords

joint sparse representation, fusion tracking, color image, infrared image

Introduction

By leveraging the combined beneﬁts of using diﬀerent modalities while compensating for failures in
individual modalities[1], multi-sensor systems oﬀer considerable advantage over single-sensor systems.
They beneﬁt from both an increased spatial and temporal coverage due to complementary sensor ranges
and data acquisition frequencies. In addition, they oﬀer an improved operational robustness, because
distinct physical sensing principles compensate for particular perception shortcomings. Altogether, they
are able to gather more information about the reality especially in varying environmental conditions[2].
As indicated by [3], the rapid developments of sensor technology, microelectronics, and communications
have led to a great need for image fusion techniques that can eﬀectively combine multi-sensor images into
an enhanced single view of a scene with extended information content. The use of color greatly expands
the amount of information that can be conveyed in a single image, giving rise to a natural approach to
the representation of multi-modal data. Refs. [4,5] also pointed out the importance of fusion of multiple
sensor information.
For fusion tracking, the choice of visual and infrared imagery is signiﬁcant, as each modal provides
disparate, yet complementary information about a scene. Infrared cameras detect relative diﬀerences in

the amount of thermal energy emitted from objects in the scene. These sensors are therefore independent
of illumination, making them more eﬀective than color cameras under poor lighting conditions. Color
sensors on the other hand, are oblivious to temperature diﬀerences in the scene, and are typically more
eﬀective than thermal cameras when objects are at “thermal crossover”, provided that the scene is well
illuminated and the objects have color signatures diﬀerent from the background[6].
There are a lot of approaches for fusion of color and infrared images. To give a comprehensive review
of this topic in this paper is impossible. So in this section only some related results are given. Ref. [7]
presents a moving object detection and tracking system that robustly fuses infrared and visible video
within a level set framework. The long-term trajectories for object clusters are estimated using Kalman
ﬁltering and watershed segmentation. Kalman ﬁltering can obtain optimal solution in the case of linear
dynamics and Gaussian noise. Unfortunately, very few practical visual tracking problems belong to this
case. Using mean-shift approach, ref. [8] proposes a framework that can eﬃciently combine features for
robust tracking based on fusing the outputs of multiple spatiogram trackers. However, mean-shift is a
local deterministic search strategy, which is easy to be trapped into local minimal, and diﬃcult to recover
from tracking failure.
The particle ﬁlter, also known as sequential Monte Carlo[9], or Condensation[10], is the most popular
nonlinear and non-Gaussian ﬁltering approach. It recursively constructs the posterior probability distribution function of the state space using Monte Carlo integration[11]. One important advantage of the
particle ﬁltering framework is that it allows the information from diﬀerent measurement sources to be
fused in a principled manner[12]. Using particle ﬁlter to fuse the color and infrared images attracts many
attentions. Ref. [1] evaluates the appearance tracking performance of multiple fusion schemes that combine information from color and thermal infrared images for the tracking of surveillance. In that paper,
some common fusion algorithms are summarized and compared, including simple and weighted averaging, similarity score product, min and max score fusion and dynamic weighting approaches. Ref. [13]
investigates the impact of pixel-level fusion of videos from visible and infrared surveillance cameras on
object tracking performance, as compared to tracking in single modality videos. Tracking has been accomplished by means of a particle ﬁlter which fuses a color cue and the structural similarity measure.
However, how to design a robust likelihood which eﬀectively incorporates multiple modal information
remains an open challenging problem.
Recently sparse signal reconstruction gains considerable interest[14]. Variations and extensions of l1
minimization have been applied to many vision tasks[15]. In these works, using sparsity as a prior leads
to excellent results. In [16], a robust visual tracking framework is developed by casting the tracking
problem as ﬁnding a sparse approximation in a template subspace. During tracking, a target candidate
is represented as a linear combination of the template set composed of both target templates and trivial
templates. Intuitively, a good target candidate can be eﬃciently represented by the target templates.
This leads to a sparse coeﬃcient vector, since coeﬃcients corresponding to trivial templates tend to be
zeros. The sparse representation is achieved through solving an l1 -regularized least squares problem.
Then the candidate with the smallest target template projection error is chosen as the tracking result.
In [17], a multi-part subspace appearance model for improving robustness is developed and an overcomplete dictionary is learned oﬀ-line. However, particle ﬁlters are used in both [16] and [17] and the
sparse representation needs to be calculated for each particle. Therefore the time cost is very large.
To tackle this problem, ref. [18] gives a diﬀerent way of treating tracking task as sparse approximation
problem. The advantages of this approach is that the sparse representation needs to be calculated only
once and the time cost is dramatically decreased. In addition, sparse representation provides a new
approach to construct the likelihood function. However, this work is restricted to single sensor cases and
the extension to multiple sensor fusion is nontrivial.
In this paper, the joint sparse presentation approach is utilized to design fusion tracking approach for
color and infrared images. This approach shares the same calculation advantage of the approach proposed
in [18] and provides better robustness. In addition, a co-learning-like approach is proposed to mutually
update the reference templates of color and infrared images. This approach dramatically reduces the risk
of drift which often occurs in self-learning updating approach.

Reference models updating using co-learning

In practical tracking scenarios, the reference template rC and rI should be updated to incorporate the
changes of appearance, illumination, and so on. Although a lot of existing update approaches can be
used to enhance the tracking performance, most of them belong to “self-learning” style algorithm. That
is to say, the reference template determined in the previous frame is used in current frame to evaluate
possible regions. Then the weighted sum of the current tracking result and the reference template is used
to get new reference template. This class of approaches usually tends to “drift” since the error may be
accumulated during the learning and tracking process. To cope with this, some scholars proposed to use
semi-supervised learning approach to avoid the drift problem, see, for example, [24–27]. However, all of
these approaches are based on classiﬁer-based tracking and cannot be applicable to our case. Ref. [28]
proposed a new promising co-learning approach to update the reference template and detector. This
idea can be utilized in our framework to design a simple yet eﬀective co-learning approach to update the
reference templates in color and infrared images. The details are introduced as follows.
At time instant k, the joint sparse representations α and ᾱ are given. According to the deﬁnition of
similarity induced by the joint sparse representation, the sample x̂jkI with jI = argmaxi=1,2,...,N {ᾱi } is
the most similar to the infrared reference template and the sample x̂jkC with jC = argmaxi=1,2,...,N {αi }
is the most similar to the color reference template. To construct the co-learning approach, the infrared
feature tjIC can be extracted from sample x̂jkC and the color feature tjCI can be extracted from the infrared
sample x̂jkI . The references can be updated by the following equation:

rC = μrC + (1 − μ)tjCI ,
(12)
rI = μrI + (1 − μ)tjIC ,
where μ is the updating parameter set by the designer. If μ is set to 1, then the reference templates will
keep ﬁxed and therefore μ can be used to control the updating speed. In the presented experiments, the
value of μ is set to 0.95.
In practice jC = jI occurs often(see points A and B in Figure 3(a)). This means that both color
modality and infrared modality select the same “best” particle sample for updating. However, this
situation does not hold for all cases (see points A and B in Figure 3(b)). This means that color modality

5

Experimental results

The proposed approach was tested on thermal/color video sequence pairs from OTCBVS dataset collection [29]. Data consist of 8-bit gray-scale bitmap thermal images, and 24-bit color bitmap images of
320×240 pixels. All images were sampled approximately at 30 Hz, and were registered using homography
with manually-selected points. Thermal sequences were captured using a Raytheon PalmIR 250D sensor,
and color sequences were captured using a Sony TRV87 Handycam[6].
In the experimental validation, all algorithms were tested on a 1.86 GHz Pentium PC with 3 GB
memory, through MATLAB implementation. For all of the experiments, the states of the particle ﬁlter
are deﬁned as xk = [xk , yk , sk ]T , where xk , yk indicate the locations of the object in registered color
and infrared images, respectively; sk is the corresponding scale. The dynamics of the tracked object are
assumed to be a random walking model, which can be represented as xk = xk−1 + vk , where vk is a
multivariate zero-mean Gaussian random variable whose variance is set by [σx , σy , σs ] = [2, 2, 0.05]. The
number of all particle ﬁlters is set to 100.
In this experiment, the tracking results of three fusion algorithms are compared: separate sparse representation (SSR), covariance tracker (CVT) and the proposed joint sparse representation (JSR) approach.
SSR is a straightforward extension of the approach proposed in [18], serving as the baseline approach.
In this approach, the sparse representation in color and infrared images are conducted separately (see
Figure 2). The likelihood function deﬁned in [18] is used to design two likelihood functions in color and
infrared images, respectively. Finally they are multiplied to form the fused likelihood function. By this
comparison the advantages of the proposed joint sparse representation approach can be shown.
Both SSR and JSR need to extract features from color and infrared frames. In color frames, color
distributions are used as object models since they achieve robustness against non-rigidity, rotation and
partial occlusion. In the experiments, the histograms are typically calculated in the HSV space using
10 × 10 + 10 bins. The resulting complete histogram is thus composed of Nh = 110 bins. In infrared
frames, each sample is resized to 20 × 10 and appearance is extracted as a 200-dimensional feature vector.
CVT is the approach proposed in [30] and a state-of-the-art tracker which can naturally fuse multiple
features or modalities. According to [30], a particle ﬁlter approach is formed whose details can be found
in [31]. Notice that the feature used in CVT is totally diﬀerent from that used in SSR and JSR. For
covariance construction, except the location, only R, G, B values in color images and intensity value in
infrared image are used.
In addition, to show the advantage of the fusion, two non-fusion approaches are compared. One is
termed as Color approach, which uses the color image only, and the other is termed as Infrared approach,
which uses the infrared image only. Both approaches use the corresponding feature and the likelihood
functions with the SSR approach.
In the experimental validation, two sequences are considered. The tracking results on Sequence I (500
frames) is shown in Figure 4. The scenes in this sequence contain a large amount of moving cloud. The
Color approach fails when the object enters into the shadow region (see yellow boxes in frames 406 and
500), and the Infrared approach mistakenly locks into the telegraph pole when the object passes it (see
cyan boxed in frames 249, 406 and 500). During frames 1–144, the tracking results of the three fusion
algorithms are satisfactory since the object does not enter the shadow region. At frame 160, the results
of CVT begins to drift and ﬁnally locks into another object (see frame 500). The results of SSR begins
to deteriorate at frame 406 and never recovers, while JSR persistently tracks the object accurately.

6

Conclusions

Joint sparse representation provides a natural and robust approach to fuse diﬀerent modal information. In
this paper, the joint sparse representation is used to design similarity between samples and the likelihood
function of particle ﬁlter fusion tracker. The proposed approach eﬀectively combines color visual spectrum
and thermal spectrum images for object tracking. In addition, a co-learning approach is proposed to
update the reference templates of both modalities and enhance the tracking robustness. Finally the
proposed fusion scheme is veriﬁed using OTCBVS dataset and is shown to outperform state-of-the-art
approaches.
